**Assistant:**

Yes — **a warrant *can* be issued for a premises even if the person being investigated does not live there or is not the subject of the warrant.**

This applies in **Canada**, including **Ontario**.

### ✅ **How it works**
Police can request a **search warrant for a place** (home, car, business, storage unit, etc.) if they have:

**1. Reasonable grounds to believe**  
that evidence of an offence **will be found at that location**, and

**2. A judge or justice of the peace approves it.**

The warrant is for the **location**, not the person.

### 📌 **Important details**
- The **owner or occupant does NOT need to be the suspect.**  
- The police must show a connection between the **premises and the alleged crime**.
- Police **do not need permission** of the owner/occupant once they have the warrant.
- The person living there will usually receive a **copy of the warrant and a list of items seized**.

### 🏠 **Examples**
- Police investigate someone for drug trafficking and suspect that they **store drugs at a friend's apartment** → warrant can be issued for that apartment.
- Police believe evidence is located at a **business premises**, even if the suspect is elsewhere → warrant is allowed.

### ❗If the warrant was improper
A lawyer can challenge:
- Lack of reasonable grounds  
- Errors in the warrant  
- If the warrant was too broad  
- If police exceeded the warrant’s scope  

Sometimes improperly obtained evidence can be excluded under **s. 8 of the Charter (unreasonable search and seizure).**

- “Someone told us maybe it’s there”  
- “We have a feeling the house is involved”  
- “We don’t know where else to search, so we’ll try this house”  

### 📌 Bottom line
Police must have **solid, factual grounds FIRST** before they can search a premises — even if the owner is not the suspect.

Yes — in **Canada**, the warrant **must include certain information**, but **it does NOT list all the evidence** police have.

Here’s how it works:

---

# ✅ **What *must* be written in the search warrant**
The actual warrant (the paper police give you) must show:

### **1. The address/premises being searched**  
– Your home, unit number, garage, car, etc.

### **2. The offence being investigated**  
– Example: drug trafficking, fraud, assault, weapons, etc.

### **3. The items they are allowed to search for**  
This can include:
- Drugs  
- Weapons  
- Phones  
- Computers  
- Documents  
- Cash  
- Anything related to the offence  

This part is usually written as:  
**“things which will afford evidence of the commission of the offence of…”**

---

# ❌ **What is NOT written in the warrant**
The detailed evidence police used to *get* the warrant does NOT appear on the copy they give you.  
That information is in a separate document called an:

### **Information to Obtain (ITO)**  
This includes:
- Surveillance details  
- Witness statements  
- Informant tips  
- Undercover operations  
- Police notes  
- Any facts used to claim “reasonable grounds”

**You do not get the ITO unless you or your lawyer request it from the court.**

Police do NOT have to show you the evidence they used to convince the judge.  
They only must give:
- the warrant  
- and a list of seized items

---

# 📌 **Why they don’t include evidence inside the warrant**
Because:
- The investigation may be ongoing  
- Informants must be protected  
- Police don’t want suspects to know everything they know  
- Some parts may be sealed by the court

---

# 🧾 **If you think the grounds were weak**
You (or a lawyer) can:

### **Request the ITO from the courthouse**
Then you can check:
- Did police follow the wrong person?
- Did they lie, exaggerate, or guess?
- Did they skip following the real suspects?
- Do the “grounds” make no sense?

If the ITO shows weak or false evidence, it can lead to a **Charter challenge**.
