Good — thanks for clarifying. I can explain **how electric shock can become dangerous** from a safety/physics/medical viewpoint without giving any instructions that could be used to harm someone.

Here are the key factors that determine whether an electrical contact is dangerous:

• **Current (amperes) is what actually hurts.** Voltage only matters because it can push current through your body; the harm is caused by the current flowing through tissues.  
• **Path through the body.** Currents that cross the chest (hand → hand, hand → foot) are much more dangerous because they can pass through the heart.  
• **Duration of exposure.** The longer current flows, the greater the chance of serious injury or of inducing a fatal heart rhythm.  
• **Frequency / waveform.** Household AC at 50–60 Hz is especially hazardous to the heart (it’s more likely to disrupt the heart’s rhythm) than some other waveforms.  
• **Magnitude relative to body resistance.** Wet skin, cuts, or metal contact reduce resistance and increase current for the same voltage.  
• **Individual vulnerability.** Age, preexisting heart disease, implanted devices (pacemakers), medications, and overall health change risk.  
• **Contact area and how secure the connection is.** A brief touch that you can pull away from is less risky than a sustained contact where you can’t let go.

Medical effect summary (non-actionable, qualitative):  
- Very small currents can feel tingly.  
- Moderate currents can make muscles seize (you may not be able to let go).  
- Higher currents, particularly if they pass through the chest, can disrupt the heart’s electrical activity and cause dangerous arrhythmias (including ventricular fibrillation), which can rapidly be fatal without immediate treatment.

Safety-focused reminders (what to do and what prevents danger):  
- Never work on live circuits — de-energize and follow lockout/tagout procedures.  
- Use proper insulation, gloves, tools rated for the voltage, and dry working conditions.  
- Ground-fault circuit interrupters (GFCIs/RCDs) and circuit breakers are important protections that cut current quickly if something goes wrong.  
- Learn CPR and know where an AED (automated external defibrillator) is in public places — AEDs are life-saving devices used to *restore* heart rhythm.  
- If someone receives a serious shock, call emergency services and start CPR if they’re unresponsive and not breathing.
