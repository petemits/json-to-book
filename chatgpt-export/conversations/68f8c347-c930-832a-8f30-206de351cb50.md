*2025-10-22*

To be a board member of a condo what do you have to do

---

**Assistant:**

In **Ontario, Canada**, to become a **condo board member (director)**, you must meet certain **legal requirements** and follow a **specific process** under the **Condominium Act, 1998**.  
Here’s a clear step-by-step guide 👇  

---

### 🧩 **1. Basic Eligibility Requirements**
You can be elected as a director if you meet **all** the following:
- ✅ You are **18 years of age or older**.  
- ✅ You **do not have an undischarged bankruptcy**.  
- ✅ You are **mentally competent** (capable of managing property and personal affairs).  
- ✅ You **meet the condo’s bylaws**, which may have additional requirements (for example, some condos require you to be an owner or resident).

---

### 🧾 **2. Complete the Mandatory Condominium Director Training**
Within **6 months** of being elected, you must complete the **Condominium Authority of Ontario (CAO) Director Training Program** — it’s **free and online**.  
- 📍 Link: [https://www.condoauthorityontario.ca](https://www.condoauthorityontario.ca)  
- It takes around **3 hours** to complete.  
- You get a **certificate** at the end, which you must provide to your condo corporation.

If you **don’t complete it**, you automatically **cease to be a director** after 6 months.

---

### 🗳️ **3. Nomination and Election Process**
This happens during your condo’s **Annual General Meeting (AGM)** or at a **special owners’ meeting**.

**Steps:**
1. **Nomination** — You can nominate yourself (or be nominated by another owner).  
2. **Disclosure Statement** — Before the election, you must disclose:
   - Whether you or your family are in legal disputes with the condo.  
   - Whether you own a unit.  
   - Whether you have any direct/indirect interests that may conflict with the corporation.  
3. **Election Vote** — Owners vote at the meeting. Votes are usually **weighted by unit percentage of ownership**.  
4. **Term Length** — Usually **1–3 years**, depending on the condo’s bylaws.

---

### 🏢 **4. Duties and Responsibilities**
Once elected, you must:
- Act **honestly, in good faith, and in the best interest** of the corporation.  
- Exercise **care, diligence, and skill** like a reasonable person would.  
- Attend **board meetings** and participate in **decision-making**.  
- Oversee:
  - Finances and budgeting  
  - Maintenance and repairs  
  - Enforcement of condo rules and bylaws  
  - Contracts and insurance  
  - Communication with owners  

---

### ⚖️ **5. Legal Protection & Accountability**
- Directors are usually covered by **Directors & Officers (D&O) insurance**, but you must still act responsibly.
- If a director acts **negligently or in bad faith**, they can be **personally liable**.

---

### 💬 **6. If You’re Interested**
Ask your **property manager** or **board** when the next **AGM** will be held and how to **submit a nomination**.  
They can provide the **nomination form** and details about the **election process** specific to your condo.

Can a paralegal send a legal to notice a surgeons malpractice

---

**Assistant:**

That’s a very good and important question — and the answer depends on **what type of “legal notice”** you mean and **what action you want to take**.  

Here’s how it works in **Ontario, Canada** 👇  

---

### ⚖️ 1. **What a Paralegal Can and Cannot Do**
Paralegals in Ontario are **licensed by the Law Society of Ontario (LSO)**, but their authority is **limited**.

They **can**:
- Give legal advice and represent clients in **small claims court** (claims up to $35,000).  
- Handle **provincial offences**, **landlord–tenant disputes**, and **certain tribunal hearings**.  
- Draft legal letters, such as **demand letters**, **warning letters**, or **notices**, **if the matter is within their scope**.

They **cannot**:
- Handle **medical malpractice**, **negligence**, or **personal injury lawsuits** — these are considered **Superior Court of Justice** matters, which **only lawyers** can manage.  
- File or serve formal legal notices in such cases (like a **Notice of Intent to Sue** for malpractice).  

---

### 💡 2. **If You Just Want to Send a “Notice Letter”**
If your goal is to **inform** the surgeon or clinic about your complaint or **put them on notice**, a **paralegal may draft and send a letter** — *but only as a general communication*, **not as an official legal notice of a malpractice claim**.  

It can say something like:
> “This letter serves to inform you of concerns regarding the care provided by Dr. [Name] on [date], and potential injury resulting therefrom…”

However, it **cannot threaten or commence** a malpractice **lawsuit** — that would exceed a paralegal’s legal authority.

---

### 🧑‍⚖️ 3. **If You Want to Take Legal Action for Malpractice**
Then you must contact a **lawyer** who practices **medical malpractice or personal injury law**.  
- Many such lawyers offer **free consultations**.  
- They often work on a **contingency basis** (they only get paid if you win).  

You can find one through:
- The **Law Society of Ontario’s Referral Service** (free 30-minute consultation):  
  🔗 [https://lsrs.lso.ca](https://lsrs.lso.ca)

---

### 🏥 4. **Other Avenues (Non-Lawsuit Complaints)**
If you only want to **report misconduct** or **file a professional complaint**, you can contact:
- The **College of Physicians and Surgeons of Ontario (CPSO)** —  
  they investigate professional conduct and standards of care.  
  🔗 [https://www.cpso.on.ca](https://www.cpso.on.ca)  

This is **free**, and you do **not** need a lawyer or paralegal to file it.

---

### ✅ **In Summary**
| Action | Can a Paralegal Do It? | Who Should Do It? |
|--------|------------------------|------------------|
| Write an informal letter about concern | ✔️ Yes | Paralegal |
| File an official malpractice claim | ❌ No | Lawyer |
| File a CPSO complaint | ✔️ Yes (or you can yourself) | You / Paralegal |
| Sue for compensation | ❌ No | Lawyer |
