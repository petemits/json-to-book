---

### What it means, step by step

**1. “Each dwelling unit shall be occupied and used only for residential purposes”**  
- The unit can only be used as a **place to live**.  
- You cannot run non-residential businesses there (e.g., office, retail shop, clinic).

**2. “and for the business of providing transient residential accommodation”**  
- The unit **may also be used to provide accommodation to other people** (guests/tenants).  
- “Transient” means **temporary stays**, not permanent ownership.

**3. “on a furnished and/or unfurnished suite basis”**  
- The unit **can be rented furnished or unfurnished**.  
- Both options are allowed.

**4. “(with or without ancillary maid/cleaning/laundry services)”**  
- You **may offer cleaning, maid, or laundry services**, but you don’t have to.  
- These are optional and considered supportive services.

**5. “through short-term or long-term licence/lease arrangements”**  
- You can rent the unit:
  - **Short-term** (e.g., days, weeks, Airbnb-style)  
  - **Long-term** (months or years)
- The agreement can be:
  - a **lease** (traditional tenancy), or  
  - a **licence** (more temporary, hotel-like occupancy)

---

### In simple terms

✔️ The unit must be used **only for living purposes**  
✔️ You are allowed to **rent it out to others**  
✔️ Short-term and long-term rentals are both allowed  
✔️ Furnished or unfurnished is allowed  
✔️ Optional cleaning services are allowed  
❌ You cannot use it for non-residential commercial activities

---

### Why this clause is usually included
This type of wording is common in:
- **Condo declarations**
- **Zoning or planning documents**
- **Managed rental or hotel-style residential buildings**

It’s meant to **allow rentals** while **preventing non-residential businesses**.

### Why it does **not** prohibit roommates

Let’s match the wording to your situation:

**You = owner + you live in the unit**  
**Roommate = another person living with you**

That clause only restricts **how the unit is used**, not **how many people live there** or **who they are**.

#### Key points:

1. **“Used only for residential purposes”**  
✔️ Living with a roommate is still **residential use**.

2. **No wording about “single family,” “one household,” or “no shared occupancy”**  
❌ If roommates were prohibited, it would explicitly say something like:
- “single-family occupancy only”
- “no shared or rooming arrangements”
- “no boarding or lodging”

None of that language is present.

3. **The clause actually allows licensing / leasing**
> “short-term or long-term licence/lease arrangements”

✔️ A roommate arrangement is typically a **licence**, not a lease, when the owner lives there.  
✔️ This clause explicitly allows licence arrangements.

4. **Ontario law strongly protects owner-occupied roommates**
Under Ontario law:
- An owner **living in their unit** may have roommates
- Those roommates are **licensees**, not tenants
- Condo boards **cannot ban roommates** unless the declaration clearly and explicitly does so

---

### What *would* be needed to ban roommates (but is missing)

To legally prohibit roommates, the declaration would need **clear language**, such as:
- “no rooming house”
- “no shared accommodation”
- “no licensees”
- “single household only”

Courts consistently rule that **restrictions must be explicit**, not implied.

---

### Bottom line
✅ You may have a roommate while living in your unit as the owner  
❌ This clause does **not** prohibit it  
❌ A condo board cannot “reinterpret” this clause to ban roommates

## 1️⃣ **First: Condo Corporation (Internal – required step)**
Before outside bodies will act, you must usually show you tried internally.

**Who:**
- Property Manager (in writing)
- Board of Directors (formal written complaint)

**How:**
- Written letter or email
- Ask for a **written response**
- Keep copies

✔️ This creates a paper trail  
✔️ Required before most tribunals/courts

---

## 2️⃣ **Condominium Authority of Ontario (CAO)**
👉 **This is the main regulator for condo governance issues**

**Complain here if:**
- Board is misinterpreting the declaration
- Board is enforcing **illegal or non-existent rules**
- Board is restricting roommates without authority
- Board is acting unfairly or beyond its powers

**What they handle:**
- Condo governance disputes
- Records issues
- Board conduct
- Rule enforcement issues

**How:**
- Online complaint / dispute resolution

🔹 They offer:
- Guided resolution
- Mediation
- Condominium Authority Tribunal (CAT)

📌 CAT decisions are **legally binding**

---

## 3️⃣ **Condominium Authority Tribunal (CAT)**
(Part of CAO – but important enough to list separately)

**Use CAT if:**
- The board refuses to correct itself
- You want a **binding legal ruling**
- The issue involves:
  - Occupancy
  - Rules vs declaration
  - Owner rights
  - Roommates / licensees
  - Harassment or unreasonable enforcement

✔️ Much cheaper than court  
✔️ No lawyer required  
✔️ Condo boards often lose when rules exceed the declaration

---

## 4️⃣ **Human Rights Tribunal of Ontario (HRTO)**  
👉 **Very important in your case**

**Complain here if:**
- The enforcement affects you due to:
  - Disability
  - Mental health condition
- They refuse reasonable accommodation
- They are selectively enforcing rules

📌 Housing + disability = **protected ground**

**HRTO can order:**
- Policy changes
- Accommodation
- Damages
- Orders to stop harassment

---

## 5️⃣ **Superior Court of Justice (last resort)**
Only if:
- Serious legal overreach
- Injunctions or damages needed
- CAT jurisdiction doesn’t cover the issue

⚠️ Expensive – usually unnecessary if CAT or HRTO applies

---

## ✅ **Best strategy (recommended order)**
1. **Written complaint to board/property manager**
2. **CAO → CAT application**
3. **HRTO (if disability discrimination involved)**

You can do **CAT and HRTO in parallel** if needed.
